Electrochemical energy conversion beyond Earth

On Earth, electrochemical cells quietly rely on gravity to separate phases, on convection to carry heat away, and on stacks that can be opened and repaired. In space and on other planets none of this holds, while every gram has to be launched. This Future Energy piece argues that architecture then moves from a downstream engineering choice to a primary design variable, and uses the monolithic gyroidal solid oxide cell as the concrete case: one printed ceramic body that makes power, fuels and oxygen without metallic interconnects.

Earth assumptions stop being free

Terrestrial systems are tuned for efficiency, through materials chemistry, assembled modular stacks and repairability, with liquid-phase cells relying on gravity-assisted separation. Beyond Earth these are replaced by mass limits, architecture-determined performance, monolithic integration, robustness and stability. The shift favours all-solid-state systems, which also avoid the radiation damage that polymer-rich cells such as PEM suffer.

The penalty of assembled stacks

Conventional solid oxide stacks combine cells, metallic interconnects, seals and balance of plant. On Earth the weight and the maintenance are managed at system level; in aerospace they keep mass-specific and volumetric power below what deployment requires. The penalty is architectural as much as electrochemical, so it calls for structural redesign rather than incremental materials optimization.

One printed body

In the monolithic gyroidal SOC, replicated gyroid units sit inside a gas-tight shell, so the gas paths stay separated with no metallic interconnect. Support can move to a dedicated structural frame, letting the electrolyte get thinner, and the all-ceramic body avoids metal-ceramic expansion mismatch under thermal shock. Performance now scales with topology, surface area, transport length and packing density, not with the number of planar cells. Details are on the Nature Energy paper page.

Mars and resource utilization

The same cell runs as a fuel cell or as an electrolyser of water and carbon dioxide, giving hydrogen, oxygen, carbon monoxide or syngas. The Mars oxygen ISRU experiment already made oxygen from the Martian atmosphere by solid oxide electrolysis. Analyses of SOC-based Mars oxygen plants indicate that monolithic architectures could cut stack mass and volume substantially and simplify balance of plant.

Open questions

The curved electrolyte-electrode interface has survived thermal-shock cycling, but long-term tests stop at about 500 h. Denser TPMS lattices add area but also transport resistance and pressure drop, so topology has to be optimized rather than maximized, with Schwarz, Schoen and Fischer-Koch surfaces as alternatives to the gyroid. Monolithic support could also make mechanically weaker proton-conducting ceramics usable at lower temperature. Architecture does not merely package materials; it decides which ones become viable.
