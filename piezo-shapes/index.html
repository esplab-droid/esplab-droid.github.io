Shape engineering and manufacturing of piezoceramics for energy conversion

For most of their history, piezoceramics were discs, plates, rings and stacks, because that is what pressing and tape casting could make. The shape still matters: it selects the vibration mode, the coupling factor and how far a device moves or how well it listens. This review follows piezoceramic geometry from multilayers, benders and flextensional transducers to tubes, spirals, helices, shells, topology-optimised elements and metamaterials, and asks what additive manufacturing now makes possible.

Shape sets the mode and the measured coefficient

Aspect ratio decides which vibration modes a piezoelectric element supports. A thin disc has a lower coupling factor (kt) than a plate (k33′) or a rod (k33) of the same ceramic. Off-standard aspect ratios mix modes and complicate impedance analysis, and the effective d33 of thin discs can fall up to 30 % below the material value.

Curvature amplifies motion

In a curved cross-section the transverse d31 strain produces a larger bending moment than in a straight beam, which is why curved benders such as RAINBOW, THUNDER and LIPCA outperform flat ones. Printed bellow tubes with zigzag walls gave up to 50 % more radial displacement than straight tubes, at the cost of 20–30 % less axial displacement.

Spirals and helices accumulate strain

A 32 mm PZT spiral printed by FDM moved 1.9 mm at about 11.6 kV cm−1, roughly twelve times a straight strip of the same dimensions. Narrow, long spirals also bring resonance down to a few hertz, into the ambient vibration band for energy harvesting; a spiral bimorph powering electronics on a beetle delivered 22 µW (1.86 mW cm−3). Helices and the double-wound super-helix (3 mm stroke, 0.2 N, 2002) extend the same effect to 3D.

concave · convexfocusing vs omnidirectional
acoustic response

Shells focus and listen

Shells and domes amplify stress and spread it more evenly. Concave shells focus ultrasound for therapy, near-field diagnostics and acoustic tweezers. Convex and spherical shells give omnidirectional hydrophones, with the highest sensitivity from thin, radially poled shells.

up to 1.2 × 105dhgh relative to bulk,
auxetic rod lattice design

Less ceramic, more response

Topology-optimised composites, cellular lattices, auxetic structures and TPMS networks raise hydrostatic figures of merit by orders of magnitude over dense ceramic while lowering acoustic impedance. Printed BaTiO3 spinodoids matched the bulk d33 with 30 % of the material and raised g33 more than tenfold. The review cautions that many designs do not yet account for manufacturability, strength or cost.

> 95 %density of conventional ceramics,
still hard to reach by AM

Printed, not machined

Vat photopolymerisation is the most established ceramic AM route and has produced PZT, BaTiO3, KNN and PMN-PT pillar arrays, annular arrays, shells, helices and gyroids. Machining of conventional parts adds stress, grain pull-out and depolarisation; AM avoids it but still struggles with defects and density. Next steps named in the review: multi-material and 4D printing, printed electrodes for tailored poling, and grain texturing as a design variable alongside shape.
