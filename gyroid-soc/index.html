Monolithic gyroidal solid oxide cells by additive manufacturing

Solid oxide cells convert fuel to electricity and back very efficiently, but they are built flat: cells, metallic interconnects and seals stacked and pressed together, with most of the weight in the hardware. We printed the electrolyte as a single zirconia gyroid. Its curved wall splits space into two interpenetrating channels, one for fuel and one for oxygen, so the wall is electrolyte, support and gas separator at once. The result is a whole cell stack in one ceramic body, with nearly an order of magnitude more power per gram and per cubic centimetre than planar stacks.

Escaping the flat stack

Planar and tubular stacks give effective electrode areas below 50 mm2 g−1 and 0.12 mm2 mm−3, and specific power below 0.2 W g−1. The metal also oxidizes and expands differently from the ceramic. Earlier 3D-printed designs, corrugated or multi-tubular, still needed thick 270 µm electrolytes and stayed close to the 2D layout.

One wall, two labyrinths

The gyroid is a triply periodic minimal surface, sin x cos y + sin y cos z + sin z cos x = 0, with zero mean curvature everywhere. Coating one side with the fuel electrode and the other with the oxygen electrode turns the whole wall into an active cell. A sealed outer shell closes the monolith, so no metallic interconnect or stack seal is needed. The sintered wall reaches ≈ 99 % of nominal density, with ionic conductivity matching commercial 8YSZ and no penetrating defects in µCT.

Power per gram and per volume

The gyroid gives six times the mass-specific and four times the volume-specific electrode area of conventional stacks. Compared with commercial planar SOFC stacks, specific power and volumetric power density rise by factors of about 7 and 9, past the 1 W g−1 threshold set for aerospace. The cells reach over 450 mW cm−2 at 900 °C, and the open-circuit voltage is 1.06 V at 800 °C.

Electrolysis, and Mars

In electrolysis the monolith produces about 7 × 10−4 Nm3 h−1 g−1 and 2 × 10−3 Nm3 h−1 cm−3, nearly ten times planar SOEC stacks. Planned oxygen production for human activity on Mars relies on planar SOEC stacks of 6,400 kg and 6,600 l. The paper estimates that integrated gyroid monoliths could deliver the same in the 800 kg and 700 l range.

Reversible and thermally robust

Monoliths ran for hundreds of hours in both modes; the SOEC degraded at 3.3 % kh−1 after its first 25 h at 800 °C. One cell switched between fuel cell and electrolysis every 10 h, another went through six 800–900 °C cycles at up to 5 °C min−1, both without failure. No delamination appeared between layers and no nickel coarsening was detected. An all-ceramic body has no metal-to-ceramic expansion mismatch.

Room to go thinner

Compression tests and finite element models show that the outer shell, not the active wall, can carry the mechanical load, and that stress spreads evenly over the gyroid with no single critical region. Thicker shells let the electrolyte drop below 100 µm, lowering ohmic loss and operating temperature, which would also open the way to cheaper silver or nickel current collectors.
