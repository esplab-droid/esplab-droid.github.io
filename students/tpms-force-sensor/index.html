Testing 3D-printed piezoelectric ceramics as three-axis force sensors for robotic grippers

When a robot picks up a glass, its fingertips need to sense both the grip's squeeze and the sideways pull as the glass begins to slip. These forces call for different responses.

Could a small, 3D-printed ceramic fingertip distinguish a normal push from a tangential force and tell the robot which way the contact is moving?

At DTU Energy, we have 3D-printed lead-free piezoelectric ceramics with curved internal walls based on triply periodic minimal surfaces (TPMS). Our recent work showed how this architecture influences motion when a voltage is applied. In this project, you will use the direct piezoelectric effect: a change in applied force on the ceramic generates charge at its electrodes. You will test whether one selected TPMS geometry, fitted with independently measured electrode channels, gives distinguishable signals for forces along three axes.

In this project you will

  1. Prepare a fingertip sensor. Mount a printed KNN specimen in a fingertip-sized holder and establish at least three independent electrical readouts. Apply repeatable contact pulses at a fixed position to check signal polarity, noise, and repeatability.
  2. Calibrate it in three directions. Apply known changes in force normal to the fingertip and along two perpendicular tangential directions. Record the charge on each channel relative to a reference force sensor. Determine the sensitivity and cross-talk, and test whether the three loading directions produce sufficiently distinct responses to recover the force components.
  3. Test it on a robotic contact task. Apply a normal preload and then pull a test object sideways or obliquely. Use the calibration to estimate the changing force vector and compare it with the reference measurement for loading directions held back from calibration.

What you will deliver. A fingertip prototype and an experimental assessment of which force components it can resolve, over what load range and with what error in direction. The core project uses a single printed geometry and focuses on changes in contact forces during gripping and at the onset of sliding.

Our team

You will work with Prof. Vincenzo Esposito and colleagues at DTU Energy on lead-free piezoceramics, ceramic additive manufacturing and electromechanical characterization.
