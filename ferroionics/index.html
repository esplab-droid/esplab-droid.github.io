Tunable ferroionic properties in CeO2/BaTiO3 heterostructures

A ferroelectric holds a permanent, built-in electric field. Ceria is a catalyst whose activity comes from its defects, oxygen vacancies and Ce3+ small polarons, which are usually activated only at high temperature. Here a 5 nm ceria layer sits on a 10 nm BaTiO3 film whose polarization is fixed up or down by the bottom interface. The ceria reproduces the buried polarization through its defects, and adsorbed water turns it into opposite surface chemistries: the surface potential differs by about 400 mV between the two cases, at room temperature and without any applied bias.

The arrow is fixed at growth

Compressively strained, single-domain BaTiO3 self-polarizes. A SrRuO3 buffer gives upward polarization, direct growth on TiO2-terminated Nb:SrTiO3 gives downward polarization. The ceria grows epitaxially on top, rotated 45° in plane, and PFM shows it leaves the BaTiO3 orientation unchanged.

Ceria mirrors the polarization through its defects

Upward polarization is compensated by Ce3+ accumulation in the ceria, downward polarization by oxygen vacancy accumulation, mainly at growth and annealing temperature. At the Ti L2,3 edge the interface appears electron-doped for Pup and hole-doped for Pdown. A small linear dichroism also appears at the Ce M4,5 edge, not reported before for centrosymmetric fluorite ceria.

Opposite polarizations, opposite water chemistry

KPFM shows the Pdown surface richer in H+ and the Pup surface richer in OH−. Heating to 200 °C, well below the Curie temperature of the strained film, removes the difference, and cooling restores it, so dissociatively adsorbed water carries the contrast. After ±5 V poling the potential stays imprinted for 12 h.

No switchingin dry argon for CeO2/BaTiO3;
bare BaTiO3 still switches

Water is part of the switch

In air, SS-PFM shows hysteresis for all films, with switching near 5 V. In a glovebox below 0.2 ppm water and oxygen, the ceria-capped films stop switching while bare BaTiO3 does not. Oxygen vacancies cannot be redistributed at room temperature, so a polarizable, electrochemically active water layer is needed to compensate the charge.

Vacancy position sets water adsorption

A vacancy at the CeO2(100) surface binds water more strongly, oxygen down towards Ce. With the vacancy buried at the BaTiO3 interface, adsorption resembles pristine ceria with two hydrogen bonds. Shifting the polarization therefore tunes water adsorption and the water ionization overpotentials within ±400 mV, pointing to wireless, field-assisted catalysis.
