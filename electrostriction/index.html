Atomically engineered interfaces yield extraordinary electrostriction

Electrostriction is the strain a dielectric develops in proportion to the square of an applied field, so it stretches the same way whichever direction the field points. In simple oxides it is tiny, below 10−19 m2 V−2. Stacking ultrathin layers of Gd-doped ceria and Er-stabilized δ-Bi2O3 with atomically sharp, coherent interfaces raises the coefficient to 2.38 × 10−14 m2 V−2, about a thousand times the best relaxor ferroelectrics. The enhancement comes from the interfaces, not from either material on its own.

A thousand times the best relaxors

The multilayers are grown on NdGaO3 cantilevers with planar gold electrodes on top. A sinusoidal field bends the cantilever, and the free-end displacement is read by laser interferometry at twice the drive frequency, the signature of a quadratic response. Simple oxides sit below 10−19 m2 V−2.

Coherent, atomically sharp interfaces

X-ray diffraction and reciprocal space maps show phase-pure, epitaxial films with smooth interfaces. Electron microscopy finds coherent CGO/ESB interfaces without dislocations: the two lattices are forced to share one in-plane spacing, so each interface stores a lattice discontinuity as elastic strain.

The interfaces do the work

Shrinking the CGO/ESB period packs more interfaces into the same film and raises the coefficient, until the layers become too thin to stay chemically distinct. Atomistic calculations trace the enhancement to coherent strain imparted by the interfacial lattice discontinuity.

Defect dipoles that steer strain

In the simulations, dopants and oxygen vacancies create local electric dipoles through off-centre ion displacements. Without a field they point every way. Under a field they align and grow, and the resulting energy change is dominated by elastic strain. Thinner periods make the dipoles easier to steer. Because each local distortion keeps its axis when the field reverses, the strain follows the square of the field.

17.4 kV cm−1cycled at 1, 50 and 200 Hz

Stable under cycling

Under a constant-amplitude sinusoidal field the response shows no degradation over repeated cycles at 1, 50 and 200 Hz. The coefficient is largest at low frequency and decreases as the drive gets faster.
